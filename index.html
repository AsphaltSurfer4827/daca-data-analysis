2026 DACA Renewal Analysis

What USCIS’s own numbers say about the current I-821D renewal delays — how large the backlog is, how long it could take to clear, and why third-party trackers can’t give you a precise date.

Abstract. A drop in approvals beginning in late 2025 — driven by the government shutdown and a new biometrics and vetting process — allowed a backlog to build to roughly 125,000 pending renewals by mid-2026. Even if applications fall back to normal levels, approvals at the recent average will not clear that backlog on their own; it would take a sustained 5–10% increase over the recent average approval rate to return to healthy levels within a few years. The July–September 2026 report, expected in early December, will be the first real test of whether that recovery is underway. All projections here are illustrative scenarios, not forecasts.

Using the four completed years (2022–2025), we should expect on average about 292,251 accepted applications a year:

(280,703 + 329,969 + 262,176 + 296,157) / 4 = 292,251

This is not flat, because DACA renewals follow a 2-year cycle (a permit lasts two years, so filers return in alternating waves). The pattern is consistent: across the last five cycles, every odd year from 2017 through 2025 drew more applications than both of its neighboring even years — odd years averaging ~357,000 versus ~278,000 for even years, about 28% higher.

Even-year levels have themselves drifted down over time (2020 was an unusually high even year at ~310,000), so the relevant baseline for 2026 is the recent even-year average of ~271,000, not the long-run figure.

That matters here: 2026 is an even year, so it should have been a lighter year for applications, not a heavier one. We can use this to project 2026’s total under four scenarios.

  1. Best Case (Non-Surge). 2026 behaves like a typical even (low) year (~271,000), which would leave only ~105,000 for the rest of the year. Likely unrealistic, since a large wave has already come in and social-media-driven panic pulled applications forward.
  2. Trough. The surge already banked in the first half stays in, and the back half runs at a normal seasonal pace rather than snapping back down.
  3. Average. The rest of the year runs at the typical blended pace across all years.
  4. Surge. The elevated first-half pace continues through the second half.

Concerning trends begin in October 2025, lining up with reports on the DACA subreddit and the government shutdown (October 1 – November 12, 2025). Against a recent-average ~73,400 approvals per quarter (the 2022–2025 mean of approvals — distinct from the ~73,063 mean of applications received), the Oct–Dec 2025 and Jan–Mar 2026 quarters came in at just 48.5% and 24.8% of average — roughly a 50% and 75% drop. In recent years, January–March runs high. Against the yearly norm of ~73,063 applications per quarter (292,251 ÷ 4):

This spring spike holds in 2024, 2025, and 2026, but not in 2023, which peaked in April–June while the program was still recovering from the earlier injunction — a reminder that the seasonal pattern is a recent one, not a law.

Biometrics & vetting

For a stretch after COVID, USCIS reused previously-collected biometrics. Toward the end of 2025 that appears to have changed — renewals now require new biometrics, which adds scheduling and clearance time before an officer can approve a case. On top of that, both law firms and USCIS posts point to a new vetting process that routes biometrics to the FBI database for extra scrutiny. Any application submitted before April 27, 2026 was effectively paused until the new check passed.

Assuming approvals recover to the recent average and hold there, we can estimate how fast the pending backlog is worked down. By attrition we simply mean the backlog being cleared out. The figures below are illustrative — they assume constant rates and a single seasonal profile, so treat them as rough scenarios rather than predictions.

USCIS always carries a balance of cases; before the delays, the low years let it clear the surge-year backlog and return to a healthy baseline just in time for the next cycle. Without a real change in throughput, the current backlog carries forward. Even in the best case — applications falling all the way back to a non-surge year — a large backlog remains.

It would take a real surge in cases reviewed to get back to a healthy level. Over 2022–2025, year-end pending sat between roughly 19,000 and 48,000 (about 30,000 on average), so we treat that band as “healthy.” Treating 2026 as a one-time surge and letting intake return to the four-year average, we project the backlog at the average approval rate and at +5% and +10%.

At the plain average rate the backlog barely moves for years.

The July–September data will be the most telling piece for these projections. Everything here depends on whether approvals actually pick back up, and the next report is the first real chance to see it. If approvals come in at or above average, the system is recovering and we can lean toward the faster timelines. If they come in flat or low, it confirms that normal capacity is not enough to dig out of this backlog and we are looking at the longer scenarios.

Anecdotally, there is reason to be cautiously optimistic. Since the April 27th change, newer cases have been approved in as little as a month. That is a big deal — it suggests USCIS may have worked through whatever was slowing the new vetting process, which could point to a surge in approvals once those cases clear in volume. It also matters how a queue like this works: if newer cases clear quickly, that frees capacity for the older cases too. Faster processing on the new applications does not just help recent filers — it opens room for the whole backlog to attrite. The Q4 numbers will tell us whether that is actually happening or whether it is still just a handful of quick approvals that have not shown up in the totals yet. That report should be available in the first half of December.

4Understanding the Limits of Approval-Calculator Tools

Like many of you, I’ve leaned on these tools to take some of the edge off the anxiety that comes with these delays. Over time that’s turned into frustration as the projected approval windows keep slipping. I’m not here to complain about the tools — I do think they still give us some useful data points. I’m more here to explain how genuinely hard it is to pin down a precise approval timeframe, and why the numbers you see should be taken with a grain of salt.

The first thing worth pointing out is that most of these tools aren’t pulling data directly from USCIS. In a lot of cases the data is crowdsourced, which can skew what we see. For example, say 100 applications get sent to USCIS and only 50 are being tracked by a third-party tool. If 25 of those tracked cases were approved within a 162-day window, that number only describes those 25. Unless that group happens to be a perfect mirror of the other 75 the tool never saw, we can’t reliably use it to guess when everyone else will be approved.

The second issue is that these tools don’t perfectly capture all the information tied to an application. In a lot of cases they don’t even know when the application was originally submitted. Some tools, like MyCasesHub, try to work around this by measuring the time between the last status update and the approval date — but even that isn’t clean, because things like a request for additional documents or a rescheduled biometrics appointment can reset or distort that window. Other estimators only use approvals where a filing date happens to be available, but that runs into the same problem: all it really tells you is that “for the applications that had a filing date, the window was X days.” And the missing data isn’t random either — from what I’ve seen, entire batches of cases simply don’t have a filing date attached, so the ones that do aren’t even a fair slice to begin with.

Something a lot of these estimates gloss over is that they can only count cases that have already been approved. Everyone still stuck waiting isn’t in the math, and those pending cases are usually the slower ones by nature. For example, if 100 people file the same day and the 40 easy cases clear in 90 days while the harder 60 are still sitting months later, a tracker only has those 40 fast approvals to work with. It’ll happily tell you the wait is around 90 days, when the real answer for most people is a lot longer. The number looks fast because the slow cases haven’t shown up yet.

On top of that, there really isn’t one single approval window — there are at least two very different pools of applications. There are the pre-policy-change cases and the post-April-27 cases, and the newer ones are getting approved much faster, which drags the blended average down. Realistically you’d want two separate numbers, one for the pre-policy applications and one for the post-policy ones, because mashing them together describes neither group. This ties into the bigger problem: USCIS doesn’t seem to follow any consistent pattern on approvals. In theory it should be a first-in-first-out pipeline, but from what we’ve seen, applications from just about any time period get mixed together — a case filed in February and a case filed in August can end up approved on the same day. More recently it looks like they’ve generally been moving through filing months in order, but even then there’s no telling how many new versus old applications get worked in any given batch.

The last thing worth keeping in mind is that when you filed matters just as much as how long ago you filed. Renewals spike hard in the spring when everyone rushes to get theirs in, so if you filed during that window you’re sitting behind a much bigger pile than someone who filed in the summer. A wait estimate pulled from one part of the year won’t necessarily apply to a case filed in another.

The clearest sign of how shaky this all is: the trackers don’t even agree with each other. For the same population and roughly the same period, daca-tracker reports a median around 202 days (80th percentile ~234), DACA Hub shows a community average near 163 days (from ~133 reports), MyCasesHub publishes about 145 days (and that one measures last-status-to-approval, a different thing entirely), and USCIS’s own reported figure is closer to 4 months (~120 days). DreamerTracker does something a little different — it imports your actual USCIS-account timeline instead of asking you to self-report — which improves the data on each case, but it still only sees cases people choose to share and only counts approvals, so it can’t escape the same trap. If any single number were reliable, these tools would converge. They don’t, and that spread — roughly 120 to 234 days for the “same” question — is the whole point.

At the end of the day, none of this means the tools are useless or dishonest. It’s that estimating a wait time from a partial, still-in-progress, self-selected set of cases is genuinely hard — and that’s exactly why it’s so frustrating to hang your hopes on any single number while you’re waiting on your own case.

5Methodology & Limitations

Data

All primary figures come from USCIS DACA Performance Data (quarterly Excel and PDF releases), fiscal years 2023–2026, cross-checked across report versions. This paper looks at renewals only; initial requests are excluded because they have been effectively nil since the 2021 court injunction. USCIS reports by fiscal quarter (fiscal Q1 = October–December); throughout this paper those are relabeled to plain calendar quarters.

Key figures

  • Average accepted applications, 2022–2025: 292,251/yr (~73,063/qtr).
  • Average approvals, 2022–2025: 293,632/yr (~73,408/qtr) — used as the “recent capacity” baseline.
  • “Healthy” pending: the 2022–2025 year-end range, roughly 19,000–48,000 (~30,000 average).
  • Seasonal shares averaged over the two comparable recent years (FY2024–FY2025); denials held at ~2,000/yr in projections.

Assumptions

Within each scenario, intake and approval rates are held constant and a single seasonal profile is projected forward. The projections are deterministic — single paths with no confidence intervals — and projected figures are rounded to the nearest 1,000. They are illustrative scenarios, not forecasts. Capacity comparisons stay within recent (2022–2026) throughput; USCIS processed higher renewal volumes before 2022, but staffing and policy have changed too much for those older figures to compare directly.

Limitations

USCIS reports lag roughly ten weeks, so the most recent quarter is always missing. USCIS also notes that pending counts cannot be reconstructed exactly from prior periods because of administrative closures and transfers, so quarter-to-quarter arithmetic is approximate. The causal explanations offered here (the government shutdown, reinstated biometrics, and the new FBI-database vetting) are plausible and sourced to USCIS notices and immigration-law firms, but they are correlational, not proven. Finally, the third-party tracker figures are crowdsourced, self-selected, and count only completed approvals, so they are cited for context and comparison, not relied upon.
